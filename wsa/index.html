KampWell

Saskatchewan observation wells, cleaned automatically

Four of the Water Security Agency's observation well records, from public data, with the barometric effect taken out by KampWell's well record cleaner, and read by checks that look for faults in each record. The same cleaner runs on the Clean a well record page. No setting was made for any well: the same checks ran on all four, and where one found that the logger's correction appears to have stopped, the response is fitted to the record before that date. The four were read by a person first, and the checks were developed on them.

Not affiliated with or endorsed by the Water Security Agency.

What was done

  1. WSA's daily average water levels for the last year plotted (May 2025 to May 2026), read from the hydrograph it publishes for each well.
  2. Air pressure for the same days from Environment and Climate Change Canada's nearest hourly station.
  3. KampWell fits each well's barometric response function: how much of a change in air pressure shows in the water level, on the same day and over the next two (regression deconvolution; Rasmussen and Crawford 1997, Butler et al. 2011). Its sum after two days is the response after 48 hours. For a confined aquifer whose response has settled by then, that is close to the barometric efficiency.
  4. That fitted part is subtracted. Only what moves with air pressure is taken out, so trends, seasons and recharge stay in the record, apart from any part of them that moves with air pressure, such as a seasonal swing in it.

For every well

  • These are daily averages, read from the points plotted in WSA's published hydrograph PDFs, because no hourly record is publicly reachable. WSA's loggers record more often. A daily average smooths over the day, so a response that comes within hours is split between the same day and the next. With hourly data the response would be resolved within the day, and the earth tide removed too. A daily average removes most of the tide, but a few per cent of it is left, showing as a slow beat of about two weeks.
  • Air pressure is Environment and Climate Change Canada's hourly station pressure at the nearest airport, 8 to 20 km away, averaged over the same day. The station's different elevation shifts its pressure by a constant, which drops out of the day-to-day changes the fit uses. WSA compensates its levels with a barometric logger of its own; that record, used instead, would remove the station distance.
  • WSA does not say which day its averages are taken over. They are matched here to the calendar day in Central Standard Time, Saskatchewan's clock all year.
  • Each well was run on the last 365 days of its hydrograph, a rule fixed before any well was run, with the tool's fixed 48-hour lag, and the same checks on every well. No setting was made for any well. These four wells were read by a person before the checks were written, and the checks were developed on them: the first version of the change check missed Regina Firehall's fault, and the weekly check was rebuilt after a review showed it gave false alarms on short stretches. So these four show what the checks find, not how often they are right; that comes from wells they had not seen. The fit has three coefficients on one year of data; its spread is shown by 95% intervals where a check reports one, and by the range across sixths of the year. There is no true answer to check these numbers against: they are what the published methods give on public data.

Check: did the logger's air-pressure correction stop from 4 April 2026?

If it did, the published level from that day is the water level plus the air pressure, plus an unknown constant. Below, the air pressure is taken back out (about its mean over that stretch, as the constant is unknown) and the record is cleaned again. See what the checks found, below, for what this shows.

The water stood about 17.7 m below the top of the casing over this year.

The response to air pressure is fitted to the record up to 3 April 2026, for the reason below. Of a change in air pressure (as a height of water), 0.25 shows in the daily level the same day, 0.55 by the next day and 0.68 after two days. The response builds with time, by 0.42 between the same day and two days (95% interval 0.41 to 0.44). That is what a well shows when it exchanges water slowly with the aquifer, through well-bore storage or a partly blocked screen (Rasmussen and Crawford 1997; Butler et al. 2011). It could also come partly from when the logger's days begin; hourly readings would tell these apart. Fitted to each sixth of the year on its own (up to the end of the fit), the response after two days ranges from 0.65 to 0.74.

The barometric part taken out has a root mean square of 4.0 cm and spans 22.3 cm over the year. Up to 3 April 2026, removing it takes 89% of the variance out of the day-to-day changes, from 26.5 to 8.7 mm (root mean square).

In the cleaned record the monthly means span 6 cm, from 559.04 m in May 2025 to 558.98 m in September 2025. Its last 30 days average 5 cm below its first 30. From October to March, when air pressure swings most, the cleaning takes the day-to-day change from 32 to 8 mm.

What the checks found

  • The record's response to air pressure changes between 4 April 2026 and 7 April 2026: the same-day response changes by 1.05 of a change in air pressure (95% interval 0.86 to 1.24; p = 0.002 for no change in the year, against the threshold of 0.01). The data cannot tell the day more closely. A change near +1 is what a record shows when the logger's air-pressure correction has stopped, through a blocked or flooded vent tube or a correction not applied in processing: the published level is then the water level plus the air pressure, plus a constant. WSA Winnipeg (Regina), cleaned against the same weather station, shows no change near then, so the station's record is not the cause. Whether WSA compensates that well and this one from the same barometric logger is not known. So the response is fitted to the record before 4 April 2026. From then the record has jumps of up to 18 cm from one day to the next, and the cleaning leaves up to 23 cm.
  • As a check, the chart under "Check" takes the air pressure back out of the published level from 4 April 2026 and cleans the whole year the same way. The response then comes out 0.25, 0.58 and 0.70, against 0.25, 0.55 and 0.68 fitted before the change, and it accounts for 62% of the variance of the day-to-day changes, against 38% for the published record over the whole year. Over the whole year without the check the response comes out 0.16, 0.47 and 0.59. That fits the reading. But even with the check, the cleaned record changes by 61 mm a day (root mean square) from that date, against 9 mm before, so something else in the record changed then too. Only WSA can say what happened.
  • The response is still changing at 48 hours, the fixed lag length the tool uses for every record: over its last day it still rises by 0.12 of a change in air pressure (16.6 standard errors, p < 0.001 for a settled response, against the threshold of 0.01). So the cleaned record keeps part of the air pressure swings that last several days. As a check, not used here, a 96-hour lag gives a response of 0.79 at its end, and a 144-hour lag 0.83. A longer lag would suit this well better; the cleaner keeps 48 hours for every record.

Running it for the whole network

The four wells above were done by one batch run: a script reads each well's level and pressure, runs the same engine as the Clean a well record page, and writes this page. For WSA's network it would run the same way on each well as its data arrive. Some of what follows is still to be built, as noted.

  1. Inputs. Each well's logger record, at the logger's own interval, and WSA's barometric logger record, or the nearest Environment Canada station where there is none.
  2. Each well's response, once. Fitted on a reference period, reviewed by a hydrogeologist, and kept with the well. Hourly data also give the earth tide, and allow a longer lag where a well needs one, as the Winnipeg well does.
  3. Every new download. The kept response would be applied to the new data, so cleaning is automatic, and refitted at intervals to check that it still holds. Today the engine fits the response on each run, flags isolated spikes and pressure gaps, and runs the checks shown under each well: a changed response and its likely cause, a response not settled in 48 hours, and a weekly pattern. Each has a threshold and an expected rate of false alarms, measured on made-up records. Keeping a response from one run to the next is still to be built.
  4. Outputs per well. The cleaned record and the removed part as a workbook with the method written out (the tool gives this now), charts like these, and a short description, updated with each run.

The engine runs in a browser or in Node, with no server and nothing to install. Each run's workbook writes out the method, the formulas and the fitted numbers, so every cleaned record can be checked.
